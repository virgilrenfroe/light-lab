Cut the beam

Mullions occlude the beam into shafts. The lens bends what gets through into the pool. Dust in the beam is scatter. An occlusion map records what blocks the light. The window frame blocks some light, and the blocked parts make dark gaps between the shafts. Radial blur streaks the light outward from the bright source. That is how the eye sees shafts in dusty air.

Where you'll see this

A cinematographer sets the f-stop. That number is the size of the hole that cuts the beam. A smaller hole lets less light through, which sets the exposure, and it keeps more of the scene sharp.

A gaffer cuts a lamp with a flag or a window. Dust and haze scatter the light that gets through, so the beam shows up as shafts.

A photographer shoots sunlight through a glass of water or a pool. The curved surface bends the beam that gets through, and the light piles up in a bright pool.
