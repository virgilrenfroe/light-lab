Crest meets crest

Two slits. Watch the waves overlap, and watch the bars on the screen.

One lamp shines on a card with two narrow slits. The light spreads as it leaves each slit. That spread is diffraction. Past the card, the two waves overlap. Where a crest meets a crest, the screen is bright. Where a crest meets a trough, the screen is dark. Those bars are interference. The distance from one bright bar to the next is about y = λ L / d. Lambda is the wavelength. L is the distance to the screen, 1.2 m on this bench. d is the slit spacing. A smaller d makes a larger y, so closer slits give wider fringes. Red light has a longer wavelength than blue light, so red fringes are wider. A wider slit narrows the broad glow that holds the bars. Cover one slit and the bars vanish. Both paths are needed. One slit leaves a single broad glow.

Where you'll see this

A CD or DVD throws a rainbow sheen because the track is a tight set of grooves. Each groove scatters the light, and each color leaves at a different angle. A lab chemist or an astronomer uses a diffraction grating for the same reason. The grating spreads a beam into a spectrum, so they can see which wavelengths are there.

A structural biologist sends X-rays through a crystal. The atoms scatter the waves. The bright spots on the detector are a diffraction pattern. From the spacing of those spots, the scientist works out the shape of the molecule.

A photographer or a cinematographer can stop a lens down to a very small opening. The opening is so small that each point of light spreads as it passes through. The picture goes soft. That spread is the diffraction limit. A smaller opening makes a wider spread, the way a narrower slit widens the glow on this screen.
