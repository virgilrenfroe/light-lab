Flat mirror. Drag the curve, or move the lamp.

The normal is the line straight out from the surface. A ray leaves at the same angle it arrived, both measured from that line. On a flat mirror the image is virtual: upright, the same size, and as far behind the glass as the lamp is in front. A concave curve brings parallel rays to a focus. Outside that point the image is real and upside down. Inside it, the image is virtual, upright, and larger. A convex curve spreads the rays and shows a smaller, wider view.

Where you'll see this

An astronomer studies the sky with a large reflecting telescope. The main mirror is concave, and it brings starlight to a focus. A large mirror can be supported from the back. A lens that large can be held only at the rim, and the glass sags under its own weight. That is why the biggest telescopes use a mirror.

A dentist uses a small flat mouth mirror to see behind a tooth. The tooth is in front of the glass. The image the dentist looks at sits just as far behind the glass. It is a virtual image.

A driving instructor has new drivers check the convex mirror on the passenger side of a car. The curve spreads the rays and takes in a wider stretch of road. The image of a car behind you is smaller than the car itself. A smaller image looks farther away than it is. That is why the mirror says objects are closer than they appear.
