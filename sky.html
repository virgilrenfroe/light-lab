Why the sky is blue

High sun. The path through the air is about as short as it gets.

Sunlight holds every color. Air molecules scatter a short wavelength far more than a long one. That is Rayleigh scattering, about one over wavelength to the fourth power. Blue, near 440 nanometers, scatters about five times more than red, near 660. That blue is the sky. Lower the sun and the path through the air grows longer, so the sun turns orange and red. The sky overhead stays blue. Larger particles scatter every color about the same. The sky goes white or grey, and clouds look white. In the glass, the side of the beam glows blue and the card at the far side turns orange. The drops are larger than air molecules. The color that leaves through the side is the color missing at the end.

Where you'll see this

A meteorologist, or an air-quality scientist, reads the color of the sky for particles. A milky white or grey veil often means larger bits in the air, such as haze, dust, or droplets. Those bits scatter every color about the same, so the blue washes out. A deep red sun means the direct beam has lost its short wavelengths. That happens on a long path through the air. Smoke can make the red deeper, because still more of the short wavelengths scatter aside before the light arrives.

A cinematographer, or a photographer, plans for golden hour. When the sun is low, its light travels through more air on the way to the set. Short wavelengths scatter out of that path. The light that remains is warm. Faces, walls, and dust in the beam pick up the orange.

A satellite remote-sensing scientist corrects each picture for the air in the way. Sunlight scatters in the atmosphere on the way down, and again on the way from the ground to the satellite. That scattered light adds a veil, and the veil is stronger in the blue. The correction takes the veil off, so the picture shows the land and the water.
